Where to camp at Glastonbury: the free camping fields

Where to pitch your tent at Glastonbury: the lively and quiet camping fields, family and accessible camping, the festival's camping tips and what to avoid.

JC

By Jonathan Connolly, who books for a group of friends every Glastonbury ticket day and built this site's tool. Facts checked 15 November 2026.

7 min read

Your Glastonbury ticket already includes camping: you bring a tent and pitch it anywhere in the festival's camping zones, from Wednesday night until Monday morning, with no early entry fee. The zones run from fields right next to the Pyramid Stage to quieter areas on the edge of the site, and different fields have very different atmospheres. This guide covers how to choose a field, the two areas set aside for families and disabled festival-goers, and the festival's own tips for setting up camp.

Everything here is from the camping, accommodation and access sections of the festival's information page, read in full on 15 November 2026. If you'd rather not carry a tent, the paid options are in Glastonbury 2027 campervans, Worthy View and tipis.

Choosing a camping field

The festival describes a few fields by name, and says camping areas are marked out on its site map:

  • Pennard Hill Ground, between The Park and The Healing Fields, is a popular, lively spot. The festival says it has a culture of its own, with people returning year on year.
  • Limekilns and Hitchin Hill Ground, on the edge of the site, tend to be quieter.
  • The west of the site, near The Park, usually has more camping space when other fields are full. Some fields are very busy and fill up quickly.
  • The extra camping space near the west car parks, added for the 2011 festival, is just over the concrete road and a very short walk to the main site.

So the choice mostly comes down to two questions: how close do you want to be to the music at night, and how much do you mind a walk? A lively field like Pennard Hill Ground means more going on around your tent. A field on the edge of the site is calmer, but further from the stages.

Two worked examples

  • A group of six who want to be in the thick of it. Pennard Hill Ground matches what the festival describes. Busy fields fill quickly, so agree a backup before you arrive: the festival says there is usually more space to the west, near The Park.
  • Two friends who want to sleep. Limekilns or Hitchin Hill Ground, on the edge of the site, are the fields the festival names as quieter. Being on the edge of the site, expect a longer walk to the stages.

If you're arriving by car, the festival also has advice for the first day: you have the energy for several trips back to your car on the Wednesday, when you're feeling fresh, but much less by Monday morning. Pack only what you can carry home again. How to get there is in how to get to Glastonbury.

Family camping

The festival sets aside two fenced fields for families with children, Wicket Ground and Cockmill Meadow, and stewards check that they're prioritised for families with children. They aren't for groups of adults camping without children. The guide to taking children to Glastonbury has where they are, what to expect and which route to take in. Paper emergency contact wristbands are available at these campsites too.

Accessible camping

The accessible campsite is at Spring Ground, on the west side of the site next to the Woodsies Tent. The camping section of the festival's information page says only festival-goers who have completed the festival's Access Application Form by 30 April 2027 can stay there. The same page's accessibility section gives a later final deadline for Access Applications, 12 May 2027, and says some facilities may reach capacity before then. So if you want a place at Spring Ground, apply as early as you can, and ask the festival's Access Team if you're unsure which date applies to you. It is staffed by stewards 24 hours a day, and the festival lists facilities including wheelchair accessible toilets, adapted showers for access customers, a Changing Places unit, charging for wheelchairs and medical equipment, a fridge for medication and a communal campfire.

Space there is limited, so the festival sets limits for each group:

  • Each registered access customer can camp with up to three other people. Personal assistants and children aged 13 or over count towards this; children 12 or under don't. For the ticket rules by age, see how old you have to be to go to Glastonbury.
  • Each access customer and their group can bring no more than two tents in total, and larger tents only when the access customer needs one.
  • Gazebos aren't allowed in the accessible campsite and will be removed.

If your group is bigger, the festival says it can camp together in a nearby public campsite, and the access customer can still use the Spring Ground facilities when needed. How to apply is in Going to Glastonbury with access requirements.

Setting up your pitch

The festival's own camping tips:

  • Find a landmark. Note where your tent is in relation to a numbered Fire Point or another fixed point, or mark your pitch with a flag. Finding your tent without one can be a nightmare.
  • Don't take up too much space, and please don't bring a gazebo. The festival says gazebos needlessly take up valuable camping space.
  • Find your campsite stewards. They have a base in every camping field, can direct and advise you, and are the first people to go to in an emergency, medical or security.
  • Say hello to your neighbours. The festival says it builds a community feeling and gives greater security. Look after each other, and get help if someone is ill.
  • Never put a BBQ in your tent or any enclosed space, even after it seems to have gone out: it can cause carbon monoxide poisoning.

The festival's terms also say fireworks, Chinese or flying lanterns, drones, flares and the burning of plastics aren't allowed.

Keeping your things safe

Tents are very vulnerable, even zipped up, and the festival points out that a padlock on a tent tells people there is something worth taking inside. Its advice:

  • If you're worried about something being stolen, don't leave it in your tent. Leave essential valuables at the free 24 hour Property Lock-ups, which are around the site and marked on festival maps with a keyhole logo. They also hand out free loo rolls and soap.
  • Mark your valuables with your postcode. UV pens are available at the Property Lock-ups.
  • The items most often lost in the camping fields are keys, wallets, phones and cameras.
  • Hide your money carefully, and don't keep it all in one place.

When you leave

The festival asks everyone to take their tent and all their camping gear home. Thousands of tents, airbeds, roll mats, chairs and gazebos are left behind each year. Pull up every tent peg too: pegs left in the ground get chopped into pieces when the fields are rotavated, and they can kill the cows that eat them.

On the way in, stewards hand you a green bag for recycling and a clear bag for general waste, and your campsite stewards provide bin bags. The festival says even five minutes of clearing up from each person makes a huge difference.

Camping off site

The festival warns about a growing number of unofficial off-site campsites. It says some are fairly priced and well run but not all of them are, that it isn't responsible for them, and that it can tell you whether a site is known to it if you contact it before you pay. Before booking one, it suggests asking:

  • What is access like between the site and the festival, and how far is the walk?
  • Is it a walking route, or is there transport?
  • Is there a well-lit, safe route to and from the festival?
  • Would you have to walk on busy roads in the dark? The festival doesn't recommend walking on the main A roads at night.

A limited number of local property owners also rent out rooms or whole houses over the festival. The festival asks anyone interested to contact its team through its contact form. Apart from its own official campsites, the festival says it can't endorse any others. The same section warns about third party "concierge" services that claim to find tickets and fail to deliver: see Glastonbury ticket scams.

What we couldn't confirm

The camping section of the information page describes only the fields above. Other parts of the page mention a few more campsites in passing without describing them, so we haven't described them either or said how fast each field fills. The festival points to its site map for where the camping areas are. We'll add anything new it publishes about camping for 2027.
