Glastonbury syndicate: how to buy tickets as a group

How a Glastonbury ticket syndicate works under the current queue: who books whom, planning a group bigger than six, keeping everyone's details ready, the resale and splitting the cost.

JC

By Jonathan Connolly, who books for a group of friends every Glastonbury ticket day and built this site's tool. Facts checked 26 October 2026.

5 min read

A syndicate is what Glastonbury fans call a group that tries for tickets together: several people queue at the same time, and whoever gets through first books for the others. Time Out describes the method as a big spreadsheet of groups of six, all trying at once. It's still the best way for a group to get in, but the 2027 queue changed what makes a syndicate work.

How a syndicate works

One person, the lead booker, can buy up to six tickets in one booking: their own and up to five more. For each person they enter that person's registration number and the postcode on their registration, and they pay the deposit for everyone. So a syndicate is really a promise: everyone queues, and whoever gets through first books the people they've agreed to cover.

Everyone in a booking must be registered with an accepted photo before the sale. Nobody needs to be online to be booked, but everyone who's free should queue, because each extra person queueing is another chance for the whole group.

What changed in 2027

Since the 2027 sale each registration gets one place in the queue, so extra devices no longer add chances, but more registered people queueing still does. Why, and what that means for your plan, is in Glastonbury ticket tips.

One open question: Time Out noted it was unclear whether someone who already has a ticket can use their registration to queue again for friends. Don't build your plan around it.

Who's in your syndicate

  • Everyone who's going and registered. Partners, siblings and the friend who's never bothered before all count.
  • Only people who are free at sale time can queue. Ask in advance who will be at a screen at 6pm on the coach sale day and 9am on the general sale day.
  • One lead booker per booking group, but every queuer must be able to book, because you can't know who'll get through.

What to collect from each member

  • Their registration number, copied from their confirmation email.
  • The postcode they registered with. If they've moved since, it's still the registered postcode that matters, unless they've updated their registration.
  • Confirmation that their photo was accepted.
  • Whether they'll be free to queue on the day.

Ask for the details as text, not a photo of the email, so they can be copied rather than retyped. Collecting registration numbers from WhatsApp shows how to ask, and messages to send your group has the wording.

Syndicates bigger than six

Split into booking groups of up to six, give every queuer the details of every group, and write down who books which group in which order. When a booking goes through, cross those people off straight away so nobody is booked twice and nobody is forgotten.

A worked example: nine friends

Say nine friends want to go together. They split into two booking groups:

  • Group A, six people: the six who most want to camp together.
  • Group B, three people: the other three.

Everyone queues. The rule they agree is simple: the first person through books Group A; the second person through books Group B. It doesn't matter which group the bookers themselves are in. If the first person through happens to be in Group B, they still book Group A first, then post in the chat so the next person through knows to book Group B.

This avoids the two most common big syndicate failures: two people booking the same six friends, and three people being forgotten because everyone assumed someone else had them.

Couples and friends who must go together

If two people won't go without each other, put them in the same booking group. Separate bookings can succeed or fail independently, so splitting a couple across groups risks one going without the other.

Spreadsheet or one page?

Most press guides tell syndicates to keep a spreadsheet of names, numbers and postcodes, and on a laptop that works. On a phone, in the middle of checkout, it's slower: cells are small, numbers get reformatted, and copying one cell at a time takes care. Use a spreadsheet to organise the syndicate, then put each booking group on one page that copies with a tap. Our free spreadsheet template is set up for exactly that.

Sharing the numbers safely

Registration numbers and postcodes aren't passwords, but they are what someone needs to book a ticket in your name. Share them only within the syndicate, not in public posts or big community groups. With Glasto Quick Copy each booking group goes on one page, and the link you send contains only first names, numbers and postcodes.

Paying each other back

Agree before the day how deposits and balances will be split. Using the 2027 deposit as an example: a lead booker buying for six paid £100 per ticket, so £600 on the day. Each of the other five then owes them £100. Settle the deposit within a few days, and agree separately how the balance in April will be handled. See deposits and balance payments.

Ask everyone to send their share before ticket day if your group trusts each other. The lead booker then isn't chasing anyone afterwards. If someone doesn't get a ticket, they get their money straight back.

If only some of you get tickets

It happens a lot: one booking group gets through and the other doesn't. Talk about this before the day, not after. Some syndicates agree to all try the resale together; others agree that whoever gets a ticket goes either way. Either is fine, as long as it's agreed beforehand.

Syndicates in the resale

The spring resale has smaller bookings. In 2025 it allowed up to four general admission tickets or two ticket and coach packages per booking, paid in full at the time. Re-plan your syndicate in fours, and check that each queuer can pay the full price for the people they'd book. See the 2027 resale.
